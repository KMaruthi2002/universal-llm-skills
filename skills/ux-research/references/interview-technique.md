# Interview Technique

Read this before writing a discussion guide or moderating an interview or contextual inquiry. It covers how to ask questions that produce evidence rather than politeness, how to probe, and how to handle sessions that go sideways.

## The anatomy of a good question

A good research question is:

- **Open**: cannot be answered with yes/no or a single word.
- **Neutral**: does not contain the answer, an emotional adjective, or your hypothesis.
- **Concrete**: anchored in a specific instance, time, or artifact.
- **Single**: asks one thing. "How do you plan and track your budget?" is two questions.
- **In their language**: uses terms the participant has used, not internal product jargon.

## Rewriting common bad questions

| Weak question | Problem | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a feature that auto-categorizes expenses?" | Hypothetical; invites politeness | "Tell me about the last time you categorized expenses. What did that involve?" |
| "Isn't it frustrating when the export fails?" | Leading; supplies the emotion | "What happened the last time you exported?" then "How did that go?" |
| "Do you like the new dashboard?" | Yes/no; preference not behavior | "Walk me through what you did the last time you opened the dashboard." |
| "How often do you use reports?" | Generalization; people over- or under-estimate | "When did you last use a report? And the time before that?" |
| "What features would you want?" | Asks the participant to design | "What's the hardest part of [job] right now?" then "What do you do about it today?" |
| "Why didn't you click the Save button?" | Implies error; puts them on the defensive | "What were you expecting to happen there?" |
| "How important is security to you?" | Everyone says "very" | "Tell me about a time you had to decide whether a tool was safe enough to use." |
| "Is this easy to use?" | Leading and abstract | Observe them using it; afterwards, "How would you describe that to a colleague?" |

## Probing

Most of the value in an interview comes from follow-ups, not from the guide's main questions.

- **Echo**: repeat their last words with rising intonation. "It was a mess?"
- **Clarify their terms**: "When you say 'approved', what does that involve?" Terms that seem obvious often hide a whole process.
- **Ask for the sequence**: "What happened next?" "What did you do just before that?"
- **Ask for the example**: "Can you think of a specific time that happened?"
- **Ask for the artifact**: "Do you have that spreadsheet handy? Could you show me?"
- **Ask about others**: "Who else is involved?" "What happens when it reaches them?"
- **Ask about workarounds**: "What do you do when that doesn't work?" Workarounds are the strongest signal of unmet needs because people paid effort for them.
- **Ask about the ideal, carefully**: "If this went perfectly, what would that look like?" Useful late in a session for understanding goals; not a feature list.
- **The five whys, softly**: keep asking why, but vary the phrasing ("What made that important?") so it does not feel like interrogation.

## Silence and pacing

- Wait 3–5 seconds after an answer before speaking. It feels long to you and normal to them.
- Do not fill pauses with suggestions ("…like maybe the filters?"). You will get your own suggestion back.
- Talk less than 20% of the time. If you are explaining, you are not learning.

## Concept and prototype reactions

If the session includes a concept, show it only after exploring the participant's current world, so their unprompted needs are on record first.

- Introduce it as a work in progress made by someone else ("the team is exploring some ideas"), so they feel free to criticize.
- Ask them to think aloud and describe what they think it is and what they would do with it, before asking what they think of it.
- Weight reactions that connect to their real episodes ("this would have helped last Tuesday when…") far above general approval.
- Treat "I love it" as weak. Ask "What would you stop doing if you had this?" or "What would you need to see before switching?"

## Contextual inquiry

Observing in context uses a master–apprentice stance: the participant does their real work; you watch, ask about what you see, and occasionally ask them to pause and explain.

- Agree on what you may observe and record, especially in workplaces with confidential material.
- Note the environment: interruptions, other people, physical artifacts, devices, notes stuck on monitors.
- Ask about deviations: "You checked the email before submitting — what were you looking for?"
- Avoid helping. If they get stuck, observe how they get unstuck.

## Remote session craft

- Test the tool and the link beforehand; send simple join instructions.
- Ask participants to share their screen when they reference their tools.
- Leave a few extra seconds of silence to account for latency.
- Have a backup channel (phone) in case of connection problems.

## Note-taking

- Separate verbatim quotes (in quotation marks), observed actions, and your interpretations (marked, e.g. with brackets or a prefix).
- Timestamp notable moments so you can find them in the recording.
- Tag notes with participant ID from the start; it makes synthesis far easier.

## Handling difficult sessions

- **Participant goes off-topic**: let them run briefly (tangents can be valuable), then bridge back: "That's helpful. I'd like to go back to what you said about…".
- **Monosyllabic participant**: switch to more concrete, demonstrable questions ("Could you show me how you…") and artifacts. Accept that some sessions are thin.
- **Participant wants to give feature requests**: note them, then dig for the underlying problem.
- **Participant asks you for help or how the product works**: "What would you expect?" first. Help only if they are blocked and the remaining session would otherwise be wasted, and note that you intervened.
- **Participant becomes upset or shares something distressing**: pause, acknowledge, offer to stop or skip, and do not push. Their wellbeing outranks the data. Pay the full incentive regardless.
- **Stakeholder observer interrupts**: brief observers beforehand to send questions to the moderator privately, never to speak in the session.
- **Wrong participant (does not meet criteria)**: finish courteously, pay them, and exclude or flag the data.

## After each session

Spend ten minutes immediately writing:

- Top three surprises.
- Strongest quotes (with timestamps).
- Anything that contradicts previous sessions.
- Changes to the guide (questions that confused, topics to add).

Keep the guide stable enough that sessions remain comparable, but fix outright broken questions immediately.
