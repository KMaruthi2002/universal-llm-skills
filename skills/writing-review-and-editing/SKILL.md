# Writing Review and Editing

Editing is serving a reader on behalf of a writer. The editor's job is to make the writer's intended meaning reach the intended audience with as little friction as possible, while leaving the piece recognizably the writer's own. Every change should be defensible in those terms: "the reader would misread this", "this paragraph buries the point", "this term changes meaning halfway through". "I would have said it differently" is not a reason.

## Scope

Covers reviewing and editing drafts of any kind: business, technical, academic, journalistic, marketing, creative, personal. Covers the levels of editing from structure down to punctuation, how to deliver edits and feedback, and how to verify the result.

Not covered:

- Verifying factual accuracy of claims, numbers, quotes, and citations — `fact-checking` (an editor should flag claims that look doubtful and hand off).
- Rewriting text that reads as generic or machine-generated into specific, substantive prose — `ai-content-authenticity`, although the editing principles here apply.
- Writing new material from scratch: `technical-writing`, `business-communication`, `copywriting`, `ux-writing`.

## Core principles

**1. Edit from the top down.** Fix structure before paragraphs, paragraphs before sentences, sentences before words, words before punctuation. Polishing a sentence that will be cut, or proofreading a section that will move, wastes effort and makes the writer attached to text that should change. It also hides the big problems: a perfectly punctuated draft with no clear point is still a failed draft.

**2. The reader decides.** The same paragraph can be ideal for specialists and opaque for newcomers. Before judging anything, establish who will read this, what they already know, what they need to do or believe after reading, and how they will read it (skim on a phone, study at a desk, hear it aloud). Most editing decisions follow from that.

**3. Preserve the author's voice.** Voice is the writer's characteristic choices: rhythm, register, humor, word preferences, how directly they make claims. Readers and writers both notice when it is flattened into generic prose. Change voice only when it fails the reader (too informal for a legal notice, too stiff for a welcome email) or when the writer asks. When in doubt, make the minimal change that solves the problem.

**4. Diagnose before prescribing.** Read the whole piece before changing anything. Many local problems have one cause: a confusing paragraph is often confusing because the section's purpose is unclear, not because of its sentences.

**5. Explain the why.** A writer who understands why a change helps can apply the lesson elsewhere, can reject it intelligently when the editor misread the intent, and keeps ownership of the work. Unexplained rewrites teach nothing and breed resentment.

**6. Do no harm to meaning.** Concision and clarity edits can silently change what a sentence claims: dropping a hedge turns "may reduce" into "reduces", merging sentences changes causality, a synonym shifts a technical meaning. Every edit must keep the meaning the author intended, or flag the change explicitly as a question.

## Process

### 1. Establish the brief

Ask, or infer and state your assumptions:

- **What is it and what is it for?** A grant proposal must persuade reviewers against criteria; a status update must inform quickly; an essay may aim to provoke.
- **Who reads it?** Expertise, relationship to the writer, language background, reading context.
- **What kind of edit is wanted?** This is the most commonly skipped question and the most common cause of friction:

| Level | What it changes | When asked for as |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental / structural | Argument, organization, what is included, overall approach | "Does this work?", "feedback", early drafts |
| Line / stylistic | Paragraph and sentence clarity, flow, tone, concision | "Tighten", "make it read better", "polish" |
| Copyedit | Grammar, usage, consistency, terminology, style-guide conformance | "Copyedit", "clean up" |
| Proofread | Typos, formatting, final errors on near-final text | "Proofread", "final check" |

If someone asks for a proofread and the piece has a structural problem, do the proofread they asked for and mention the structural issue briefly; do not reorganize their document uninvited.

- **What constraints apply?** Word or page limits, a style guide (house guide or a published one such as AP, Chicago, APA, MLA, a company docs style guide), spelling variety (US, UK, other), required sections, legal or brand-mandated wording that must not change, deadlines.
- **How should edits be delivered?** Tracked changes, comments, a revised version plus summary, a list of suggestions. Match the writer's tools and workflow.

### 2. Read the whole thing first

Read once without editing, as the target reader would. Note:

- What you think the main point is. Then check whether the writer would agree. If you cannot state it in a sentence or two, that is the first finding.
- Where you got lost, bored, skeptical, or confused, and where you had to reread.
- What works well. You need to know what to protect.

### 3. Structural edit

Questions that find structural problems:

- **Is the point clear and early enough for this genre?** Reports, memos, emails, and most nonfiction for busy readers benefit from stating the conclusion or request up front. Essays and narrative may legitimately build toward it. Academic papers follow their discipline's conventions.
- **Does every section serve the purpose?** Mark sections that could be cut without loss. Writers often include material because it was work to produce, not because the reader needs it.
- **Is the order the reader's order?** The writer's chronology of discovery ("first we tried X, then Y") is rarely the reader's order of need ("what works, why, what to do").
- **Is anything missing?** Unanswered obvious questions, unaddressed counterarguments, missing definitions, missing next steps, claims without support.
- **Is the proportion right?** Space should track importance. A page on background and a paragraph on the recommendation is a proportion problem.
- **Do headings, if any, let a skimmer navigate and get the gist?** Read only the headings and first sentences of paragraphs; does the argument survive?

A reverse outline is the most reliable structural tool: write down the one-line point of each paragraph, then look at the list. Duplicates, gaps, wandering order, and paragraphs with two points become obvious.

Deliver structural edits as recommendations with reasons, usually as a short memo or comments, because the writer must make the decisions. Only restructure directly if asked.

### 4. Paragraph and line edit

**Paragraphs.** One main idea each, usually announced or clearly implied early. Transitions should express the actual logical relation (contrast, cause, example, sequence) rather than generic connectors. Very long paragraphs hide their point on screens; very short ones in a row fragment an argument.

**Clarity in sentences.** Common, fixable sources of unclear prose:

- **Buried actors and actions.** "Implementation of the review of the policy was undertaken by the committee" becomes "The committee reviewed the policy." Look for nominalizations (abstract nouns made from verbs: implementation, consideration, utilization) and turn them back into verbs with clear subjects.
- **Long distance between subject and verb.** Readers hold the subject in memory until the verb arrives. Move intervening clauses.
- **Old before new.** Sentences read best when they start with what the reader already knows and end with new information, which also links sentences into a chain.
- **Ambiguous references.** "This", "it", and "they" with several possible antecedents. Add the noun: "this delay".
- **Stacked nouns.** "Customer data retention policy review timeline" forces the reader to parse backwards.
- **Negatives and double negatives.** "Not uncommon", "unless it is not" — state the positive where possible.
- **Undefined jargon and acronyms** for the given audience. Define on first use or replace.
- **Passive voice** when it hides who is responsible, or makes a sentence harder. Passive is fine, even better, when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when keeping the topic in subject position helps flow. Do not mechanically remove all passives.

**Concision.** Cut words that do no work, but stop when cutting starts to remove meaning, nuance, or voice.

- Redundant pairs and modifiers: "each and every", "end result", "completely finished", "past history".
- Throat-clearing openings: "It is important to note that", "In today's world", "When it comes to".
- Weak verbs plus nouns: "make a decision" (decide), "conduct an analysis" (analyze).
- Hedge stacks: "may possibly suggest" — keep one hedge if uncertainty is real, none if it is not.
- Restating the previous sentence, or summarizing a short section at its end.

A rough diagnostic: if a sentence can lose a third of its words with no loss of meaning, it was padded. If removing words requires the reader to infer something, it was not.

**Tone and register.** Check fit with audience and purpose: formality, warmth, confidence, directness. Watch for unintended tone: passive-aggressive phrasing in emails, condescension in explanations ("simply", "just", "obviously"), overclaiming in persuasive writing, apology spirals. In cross-cultural or second-language contexts, prefer plain constructions and avoid idioms that do not translate.

**Rhythm.** Vary sentence length. Several long sentences in a row tire the reader; many short ones sound clipped. Reading aloud (or imagining it) is the fastest way to catch both.

### 5. Copyedit for consistency and correctness

- **Terminology:** one term per concept throughout. Switching between "user", "customer", and "client" makes readers wonder whether they are different people. Technical and legal documents demand this strictly.
- **Style conformance:** capitalization, numbers (numerals vs words), dates, units, abbreviations, serial comma, hyphenation, quotation marks, list punctuation — follow the specified style guide, or, if none, the document's own dominant pattern. Build a short style sheet as you go (decisions and their first occurrence) so choices stay consistent across a long document and between editors.
- **Grammar and usage:** agreement, tense consistency, dangling modifiers, parallel structure in lists and comparisons, commonly confused words (affect/effect, fewer/less in formal registers, its/it's, principal/principle).
- **Cross-references:** figure and table numbers, section references, footnotes, links, names of people and products, consistent spelling of names.
- **Lists and headings:** parallel grammatical form, consistent capitalization, logical numbering.
- **Inclusive and precise language:** person-first or identity-first language as the relevant community and style guide prefer; avoid unnecessary gendered terms; avoid language that stereotypes. Follow the author's and publication's policy; raise questions rather than imposing.

Do not "correct" deliberate choices: dialect in dialogue, a brand's chosen capitalization, a stylistic fragment, regional spelling, legitimate usage that some prescriptive rule dislikes (split infinitives, ending a sentence with a preposition, starting with "and" or "but" are standard English).

### 6. Flag what you cannot fix

Mark, rather than silently change:

- Claims that look factually doubtful, numbers that do not add up, quotes without sources, citations that look wrong. Hand off to `fact-checking` or ask the author.
- Possible legal, defamation, privacy, or confidentiality issues.
- Places where you are unsure what the author meant. Ask: "Do you mean X or Y here?" Guessing produces confident wrong edits.

### 7. Proofread last

Proofreading is a separate pass on near-final text, ideally in the final format, because layout introduces its own errors. Techniques that work because they defeat the brain's habit of reading what it expects:

- Read slowly, aloud or with text-to-speech.
- Read in a different format or font than you edited in.
- Do targeted passes for one thing at a time: numbers and dates; names; headings and captions; links; punctuation at line ends; repeated words ("the the") at line breaks.
- Check what spellcheckers miss: correctly spelled wrong words (form/from, manger/manager, public/pubic), homophones, wrong-but-valid names.
- Check the edges: title, headers and footers, table of contents, captions, alt text, metadata, email subject line.

## Diagnosing by symptom

Reader reactions point to causes at particular levels. When you notice the symptom, look at the level indicated before reaching for sentence fixes:

| Symptom while reading | Usual cause | Level to fix |
|---|---|---|
| "What is this for?" after the first page | Purpose or main point not stated, or stated late | Structure |
| "Didn't we cover this?" | Repetition across sections; missing reverse outline | Structure |
| "Wait, how did we get here?" | Missing step in the argument; unexplained jump | Structure / paragraph |
| "So what?" | Facts without interpretation or implication | Paragraph |
| Rereading a sentence to parse it | Buried verb, long subject, stacked nouns, ambiguous pronoun | Sentence |
| Skimming without absorbing | Padding, uniform rhythm, no signposting | Line |
| "Is this the same thing as before?" | Inconsistent terminology | Copyedit |
| Distrust or irritation | Overclaiming, tone mismatch, condescension, errors | Line / copyedit / proofread |
| Stopping on a typo | Proofreading | Proofread |

The table also helps when giving feedback: describe the symptom you experienced, then the cause you suspect. The writer can then judge whether your diagnosis fits their intent.

## Delivering edits

- **Tracked changes and comments** (word processors, collaborative editors) suit most documents: the writer sees every change and can accept or reject. Keep comments for questions and reasons; do not use comments to make dozens of tiny mechanical points that could simply be tracked changes.
- **Suggestion lists** suit code-hosted docs, plain text, or cases where you cannot edit the source: quote the original snippet, give the proposed text, and the reason in a few words.
- **A revised version plus a change summary** suits requests for a finished product. The summary lists the structural changes and recurring patterns ("cut the background section to two paragraphs; standardized 'customer' throughout; converted passive constructions where the actor mattered") so the writer can check them.
- **An editorial letter or memo** suits developmental edits: the big picture, what works, the main problems in priority order, and options for addressing them.

Whatever the format, make the scale of change visible. A writer who receives a version with every sentence altered and no indication of why cannot review it efficiently and may reject it wholesale.

## Worked example: a line edit with reasons

Original (from an internal report, for executives):

> It is worth noting that, following a comprehensive analysis of the data that was conducted by the team over the course of the last quarter, it has been determined that there may potentially be an opportunity for the reduction of support costs through the implementation of a self-service portal, which could possibly reduce ticket volume by approximately 30%.

Diagnosis, in order of importance:

1. The point (a portal could cut ticket volume by about 30%) arrives at the very end, after 40 words of process. Executives skimming will miss it.
2. Actions are buried in nouns: analysis, determination, reduction, implementation.
3. Four hedges ("may", "potentially", "could possibly", "approximately") for one uncertain estimate. One honest hedge is enough; the stack reads as evasive.
4. "It is worth noting that" and "over the course of" are filler.

Edited:

> A self-service portal could reduce support tickets by about 30%, cutting support costs, according to the team's analysis of last quarter's data.

What was kept: the claim, its uncertainty (one hedge, "could", plus "about"), the source of evidence, and the neutral register. What to ask the author: "Is 30% a point estimate or the middle of a range? If you have the range, executives will want it." That is a substance question, not a style one, and it belongs in a comment.

## Long and multi-author documents

- Do a structural pass across the whole document before anyone line-edits individual sections, or each section gets polished in isolation and the whole still does not cohere.
- Agree on a style sheet early (terms, capitalization, numbers, tone) and share it with all contributors.
- Expect voice to differ between authors. Decide with the owner whether to unify it (usually yes for reports and docs, often no for anthologies or interviews) and to what.
- Check the joins between sections: duplicated introductions, contradictory figures, terms defined twice differently, forward references to content that moved.
- Track open questions in one place, each with an owner.

## Editing your own writing, or a generated draft

Self-editing fails because you read what you meant, not what you wrote. Create distance: wait before editing if time allows, change the format, read aloud, and do a reverse outline as if it were someone else's work. Ask the brief questions of your own draft explicitly.

Drafts produced by a language model need the same levels of edit plus particular attention to: claims and citations that may be invented (hand to `fact-checking`), generic filler and formulaic structure (see `ai-content-authenticity`), confident statements that should be hedged, and content that answers a slightly different question from the one asked. Editing such a draft is an editing job like any other; the standard is whether the result is accurate, specific, and serves the reader.

## When the writer disagrees

Writers reject edits, and they are often right: they know their audience, their intent, and constraints the editor does not. When an edit is rejected:

- If the issue is preference, let it go.
- If the issue is a reader problem (ambiguity, an error, a likely misreading), explain once, concretely, with the reader's likely interpretation, then accept the writer's decision.
- If the issue is a factual, legal, or ethical problem, escalate to whoever owns the publication, rather than arguing or silently overriding.

## Readability scores and tools

Readability formulas (Flesch-Kincaid and similar) count sentence length and syllables. They can flag a document that is much harder than its audience can handle, but they say nothing about structure, logic, terminology, or whether the content makes sense, and optimizing for them produces choppy prose. Grammar and style checkers catch many mechanical errors and propose many wrong "fixes". Use both as prompts for human judgment, never as the decision.

## Ethics of editing

Editing a person's work for clarity is normal and legitimate in nearly every context. Writing substantive content that a person will submit as their own work where originality is assessed (coursework, exams, some academic and professional submissions) may not be, depending on the rules that apply to them. When a request crosses from editing into producing graded or attributed original work, say what the line is under typical policies, and help in ways that stay on the right side of it: explanations, feedback, examples on other material.

## Giving feedback vs rewriting

| Do it yourself (edit directly) | Give feedback (let the writer revise) |
|---|---|
| Mechanical fixes: typos, grammar, consistency, style guide | Structural changes: order, scope, argument |
| The writer asked for a finished, polished version | The writer is learning, or the piece is a draft |
| The fix is unambiguous and small | The fix depends on the writer's intent or knowledge |
| Tight deadline and the writer approves direct edits | The change affects voice or tone substantially |

When rewriting a sentence or paragraph, keep as many of the writer's words and rhythms as possible, and show the change (tracked changes or before/after) rather than replacing silently. If you produce a full rewrite on request, still explain the main changes so the writer can review them.

Good feedback is:

- **Specific and located:** "In paragraph 3, the claim that costs fall by half needs a source or a softer verb", not "needs more evidence".
- **About the reader's experience:** "I lost the thread here because..." is easier to act on and harder to dismiss than "this is confusing".
- **Prioritized:** separate the two or three changes that matter most from the optional suggestions. Twenty equally weighted comments overwhelm.
- **Balanced honestly:** name what works and why, so it is kept; do not invent praise.
- **Framed as suggestions with reasons,** leaving decisions with the writer.

## Working with different kinds of text

- **Business writing:** lead with the conclusion or request; make action items, owners, and dates explicit; cut background the reader already knows. See `business-communication`.
- **Technical documentation:** accuracy, consistent terminology, task-oriented structure, steps that can be followed literally, code and commands verified. See `technical-writing`.
- **Academic writing:** follow the discipline's structure and citation style; preserve hedging that reflects real uncertainty; check that claims match the evidence cited; do not change technical terms. Editing for clarity is legitimate; writing the scholarship for the author is not — respect the institution's policies on editing help.
- **Marketing and web copy:** clarity of offer, audience benefit, scannability, brand voice guidelines; flag unsupported claims (legal risk). See `copywriting`.
- **Fiction and creative nonfiction:** voice, character consistency, pacing, point of view, continuity (names, timelines, details). Edit with a lighter hand on style; rules bend for effect.
- **Translations and second-language writers:** distinguish errors from legitimate variation; prefer clarity over idiomatic flourish; check that edits do not introduce meanings the writer cannot evaluate, and explain changes in plain terms.
- **Short-form (emails, messages, posts):** the subject or first line carries most of the weight; one purpose per message; check tone twice.

## Editing for a wide audience

When the readership is broad, international, or includes people reading in a second language or with cognitive or visual disabilities, add these checks:

- Plain language for anything people must act on: common words, one idea per sentence, the action stated directly ("Send the form by 3 June"), not implied.
- Headings that describe content, so readers and screen reader users can navigate by them; real heading structure rather than bold text.
- Link text that makes sense out of context ("Download the 2025 annual report (PDF, 2 MB)") rather than "click here".
- Idioms, sports metaphors, and culturally specific references replaced where they would confuse.
- Dates in an unambiguous format (03/04 means different days in different countries), units with conversions where readers vary.
- Alt text and captions for images that carry meaning; tables that make sense when read row by row.

These are editing concerns, not only design ones; see `accessible-design` and `ux-writing` for interface text.

## Discovering context

- Look for a style guide, previous publications, brand voice guidelines, templates, or past edited documents from the same author or organization. Their conventions override general preferences.
- Infer the writer's voice from the parts they clearly wrote with care; edit the rest toward that, not toward your defaults.
- Check the publication or venue requirements (journal guidelines, word limits, grant criteria) and edit against them.
- Ask about non-negotiables: legally approved wording, quotations, product names, terms defined in contracts.

## Pitfalls

- **Line editing a structurally broken draft.** The writer fixes commas and the piece still fails.
- **Homogenizing voice.** Everything comes out sounding like the editor, or like generic corporate prose.
- **Meaning drift.** Cuts and rephrasings that change claims, remove necessary hedges, or alter technical meaning.
- **Over-editing.** Changing correct text to your preference, especially at proofread stage; this introduces new errors and erodes trust.
- **Rule worship.** Applying zombie rules (never split infinitives, never use passive, never start with "because") as if they were errors.
- **Inconsistent edits.** Fixing a pattern in one place and not the others. Use search to apply consistency decisions throughout.
- **Feedback without priority,** or only negative feedback, or feedback about the writer instead of the text.
- **Ignoring the format.** Editing a document's text without checking how it renders (tables, headings, slides, mobile email).
- **Introducing errors.** Every edit is a new chance for a typo or broken sentence; reread every changed sentence in context.
- **Silently "fixing" facts.** Changing a number or name because it looks wrong, without checking or asking.

## Quality bar and verification

Before returning edits:

- [ ] The level of edit matches what was asked, and any larger issues found are flagged briefly.
- [ ] The main point is clear and positioned appropriately for the genre and reader.
- [ ] Every structural recommendation has a reason tied to the reader or purpose.
- [ ] Every changed sentence preserves the intended meaning; questions replace guesses.
- [ ] Terminology and style decisions are consistent throughout (search for variants).
- [ ] The author's voice is still recognizable; changes to voice were requested or justified.
- [ ] Doubtful facts, numbers, quotes, and citations are flagged, not silently altered.
- [ ] Edits are delivered in the format the writer can use, with significant changes visible and explained.
- [ ] A final proofread pass was done on the final version, including headings, captions, and links.
- [ ] Re-read the edited version straight through as the target reader: it should be easier to read than the original, not merely different.
